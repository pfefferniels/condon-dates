Where the date of punching is written on each of Stanford's red Welte rolls, what it says, and which perforator cut the copy.

Dated copies, by the pitch their perforator cut

What this table does and does not settle

  • An earlier version of this page said the change fell in thirteen weeks of 1914. That was wrong and is withdrawn. It rested on nineteen dated copies, among which no wide-punch roll happened to be dated after January 1914. With 220 the absence disappears. None of the individual readings was wrong; the inference from them was.
  • The pitch, not the diameter, is what is measured here. It is a slot plus the bridge after it. The grey level chosen for an edge moves those two against each other and leaves their sum alone, so a pitch compares across scans where a diameter does not. The Teilung holds at 3.19 mm in every cluster, so none of them is a difference of lateral scale.
  • Two rolls carry the overlap on their own. Welte 1697 reads "24. 8. 11" and has a 2.500 mm pitch — the narrow machine, in August 1911, two and a half years before roll 225's own copy of January 1914 at 3.00 mm. Welte 1275 reads "1275. 25. 4. 22." with a signature and has a 3.015 mm pitch — the wide machine, in April 1922. Both were measured twice, independently.
  • Four clusters, not two. 2.50 and 3.01 mm hold most of the archive; 2.60 mm holds 77 rolls and 2.83 mm holds 24. The 2.83 group is dated 1904 to 1909 and never later, which makes it either an earlier setting of the wide machine or the oldest paper having shrunk about six per cent along its length. A constant Teilung rules out shrinkage in both directions at once, not shrinkage along the roll alone.
  • The counts are a collection, not a production record. These are the copies Denis Condon happened to gather. That before 1911 almost everything is wide and after 1920 almost everything is narrow may describe what Welte ran or what survived; these data cannot separate the two.
  • Some dates are conventions. A bare digit string is split by the rule that "14114" is 14.1.1914. Those readings are marked medium or low. Two rolls are flagged for a wider crop before anything rests on them: ng103jj1150, whose year could be 3, 5 or 8, and gg384dv5303, whose written number does not match its catalogue number.

These regions are suggestions; the readings are provisional. Each scan was searched end to end for ink on paper — not only the end margin — and every patch scored on how much ink it holds, how far inside the paper edge it sits and how far past the last perforation. The best-scoring patch of each roll is pictured; the others are listed by box and reason. Three things imitate an inscription and are scored down: creases running along the roll, damp stains, and the printed leader.

  • The writing runs in both directions along the roll, so every crop carries the picture twice, the second turned 180 degrees. One of the two reads.
  • An inscription is usually three things: the roll's own number, the date, and the hand that punched it — "660. Fritz 7 Dezb 1908". Where the number on the paper matches the catalogue, the reading is anchored to the right roll.
  • A date built on grouping rather than separators — "2424" for 2.4.24 or 24.2.4 — is marked low and should not be used.
